# The 2026 Ebola Outbreak: Where Things Stand

**A complete guide to the epidemic in the Democratic Republic of the Congo, the first case in Kenya, and what is being done about it.**

*Last updated: 7 October 2026*

---

## The short version

An Ebola outbreak has been spreading in the eastern Democratic Republic of the Congo since it was declared in May 2026. As of the most recent government figures, **8,603 people have been confirmed infected and 4,148 have died** — a fatality rate of roughly 48%, meaning about one in two confirmed patients does not survive.

It is the **second-deadliest Ebola outbreak ever recorded** and the fastest-growing. Only the 2013–16 West Africa epidemic, which killed more than 11,000 people, has been worse.

Two things make this outbreak unusually difficult. The first is the virus itself: this is a **rarer species of Ebola than the one most vaccines and drugs were built for**, which means there is no licensed vaccine and no approved treatment for it anywhere in the world. The second is where it is happening: the affected provinces have an active armed conflict, more than a million displaced people, and a health workforce that has gone months without pay.

On **6 October, Kenya confirmed its first Ebola case in history** — a man who had been living in Congo, travelled overland to Uganda, flew to Nairobi, and died two days after being admitted to hospital. No one he came into contact with has tested positive so far.

## Part 1: What this virus is

### Ebola is not one virus

"Ebola" is a family of related viruses. Four of them can make people sick. The one most people have heard of — the one behind the 2014 West Africa epidemic and most outbreaks since — is called **Zaire ebolavirus**.

This outbreak is caused by a different member of the family: **Bundibugyo virus**, often shortened to BDBV. It was only identified in 2007, and before this year it had caused just two known outbreaks, both small.

This distinction sounds technical. It is the single most important fact about the outbreak, and here is why.

### Why the species matters so much

Nearly every tool the world built for Ebola was built for the Zaire species.

- **The vaccine.** Ervebo, the licensed Ebola vaccine, protects against Zaire ebolavirus. **It is not licensed for Bundibugyo**, and it is genuinely unknown whether it protects against it. There is some laboratory evidence of partial cross-protection and some animal evidence that is mixed at best — in one review of animal studies, three of four treated animals survived but still became visibly ill.
- **The drugs.** Two antibody treatments, sold as Inmazeb and Ebanga, are approved for Ebola and have been shown in a proper clinical trial to improve survival. **Both work only against the Zaire species.** Neither is approved or being used here.
- **The tests.** The most widely installed Ebola test across central and east Africa, the GeneXpert Ebola assay, looks for genetic sequences specific to Zaire ebolavirus. **It does not reliably detect Bundibugyo.** Early in this outbreak, patients who actually had Ebola tested negative. Until a replacement was authorised, the only way to confirm a case was to ship the sample to a national laboratory in Kinshasa — days away.

So the outbreak began with the world's entire Ebola toolkit pointed at the wrong target.

### How it spreads, and how dangerous it is

Ebola spreads through direct contact with the blood, vomit, diarrhoea, sweat, saliva, or other bodily fluids of someone who is sick or has died of it. It is **not** airborne. You cannot catch it from someone who has no symptoms.

This is why two activities drive most transmission: **caring for a sick family member at home**, and **washing and preparing a body for burial** — a practice of deep cultural importance across the region, and one of the most dangerous moments in an Ebola epidemic, because a body is at its most infectious just after death.

It is also why the response focuses so heavily on two things that families often experience as intrusions: moving the sick into isolation units, and having strangers in protective suits take away the dead.

The fatality rate in this outbreak is around **48% of confirmed cases**. For young children it is far worse: **roughly seven in ten confirmed cases in children under five have died.**

One caveat on that 48% figure worth understanding: during an active outbreak, fatality rates are rough. Some patients counted today are still in hospital and their outcome is unknown. And because mild cases are more likely to go undetected than fatal ones, the true proportion of all infected people who die is probably somewhat lower than the confirmed-case figure suggests.

### A note on why the numbers disagree

If you follow this outbreak across different news sources, you will see different totals on the same day. This is not sloppiness, and it is worth understanding.

The Congolese government, the World Health Organization, and the US CDC each publish figures, and they lag each other by days. On 2 October, Congo reported 8,300 cases and 4,018 deaths; WHO's count for the same day was 8,463 and 4,082. The European health agency notes plainly that Congo's figures are under continuous review and revision.

Weekly trend numbers are even shakier. Different organisations publish weekly case counts for late September that range from under 300 to nearly 500, because they are counting different things — some by the date a patient first got sick, others by the date a case was reported. **Treat any single weekly percentage you see about this outbreak with caution, including the ones further down this page.**

Part of that fatality gap is probably real: fewer treatment beds, more patients arriving too late, more deaths at home. Two of the newest affected districts sit on international borders: **Bulu**, on the Central African Republic frontier, and **Dungu**, on the South Sudan frontier. That geography is now the main thing regional health authorities are watching.

**There are no confirmed cases in Kinshasa**, Congo's capital of some 17 million people. That remains the worst realistic scenario and has not happened.

## Part 3: Kenya's first case

### What happened

On **Tuesday 6 October**, Kenya's Ministry of Health announced the country's first confirmed Ebola case in its history.

The patient was a Kenyan citizen who had lived in the Democratic Republic of the Congo for seven years. He had been ill for roughly a month and had been treated at several Congolese hospitals without a diagnosis. He then travelled **overland to Kampala, Uganda**, and boarded a flight to Nairobi, **landing at Jomo Kenyatta International Airport at 1:10pm on Saturday 3 October.**

He went to **The Nairobi Hospital** with fever, chills, exhaustion, muscle pain, a sore throat, difficulty swallowing, and bleeding under the skin at injection sites. Staff isolated him immediately in the emergency department and moved him to a dedicated isolation facility, which the hospital says is a physically separate building with its own entrance and its own staff. He **died on the evening of Monday 5 October**, about two days after admission.

Two Kenyan laboratories — the National Virology Reference Laboratory and KEMRI, the national medical research institute — confirmed Bundibugyo virus, with results also shared with the US CDC.

### Who has been traced, and what has been found

Contact tracing moved quickly and the numbers grew as it went:

- **6 October:** 28 contacts being monitored — family members and hospital staff — plus 23 passengers and 4 crew from his flight being traced.
- **7 October:** **57 contacts traced, 10 of them in quarantine.**

Contacts are monitored for 21 days, the maximum time between exposure and symptoms, and released after a negative test.

**No one else has tested positive.** Kenya has now tested between 267 and 276 suspected samples nationwide, all negative.

### What Kenya is doing

Kenya had been preparing for months. By 6 October it had screened **652,584 travellers** and trained **4,971 health workers**, with 15 border crossings and airports under heightened surveillance and 23 isolation facilities ready. Nairobi County alone reports 923 health workers and 1,598 community health promoters trained, and rapid response teams in all 17 of its sub-counties.

After the case was confirmed, Health Cabinet Secretary Aden Duale announced **enhanced screening at Nairobi's main airport for arrivals from ten countries** — Congo, Uganda, Tanzania, Ethiopia, Angola, Zambia, Rwanda, Burundi, South Sudan and the Central African Republic — routed through a single gate with health declaration forms. Five facilities near the Uganda border are being designated as temporary isolation centres, and a district hospital in Busia County is being renovated as a first point of care.

WHO delivered about 1,000 Ebola tests and 1,000 sets of protective equipment to high-risk Kenyan counties. The Africa CDC sent a team to Kenya on 6 October. WHO Director-General Tedros Adhanom Ghebreyesus stated that the organisation **advises against travel or trade restrictions** on the affected countries.

### How worried should anyone be?

**Less than the headlines suggest, with one real caveat.**

The reassuring part: a single imported case, caught and isolated on arrival at hospital, with no onward transmission four days later, in a country that had screened 650,000 travellers and trained 5,000 health workers, is a health system working. Kenya detected, isolated, confirmed, and traced. Ebola is also not an easy virus to catch — no casual contact, no airborne spread — which is why single imported cases have repeatedly been contained in the past, including in Nigeria in 2014 and in several European countries during this outbreak.

The caveat is the journey, not the destination. This man was visibly ill for a month. He crossed an international border by road, passed through a second country, boarded a commercial flight, and cleared airport screening in a third country — and was identified only when he walked into a private hospital. Kenyan MPs are now asking publicly how he got through, and that is a fair question. No Ugandan contact-tracing figures for his road transit have been published at all.

The honest summary is that **border screening is a weak net**. It catches people with obvious fever at the moment they are checked. It does not catch someone chronically ill who takes paracetamol before a flight. The thing that actually caught this case was a hospital with an isolation protocol and staff who recognised what they were seeing.

### Other countries

Five other countries have recorded exported cases from this outbreak. **None has had any onward spread.**

- **France:** one case in June, a doctor working with a humanitarian organisation. Recovered.
- **Germany:** two patients medically evacuated, in May and July.
- **United Kingdom:** one resident evacuated and treated in London in July.
- **Netherlands:** a health worker who tested positive on 29 September and was evacuated to Leiden on 1 October.
- **Kenya:** the case described above. Fatal.

**Uganda's own outbreak is over.** It was declared ended in late August after 42 days with no new cases — the standard waiting period, twice the maximum incubation time. Uganda had 20 confirmed cases and 2 deaths. Fifteen of those cases were imported from Congo; five were locally transmitted. For a country sharing a long and busy border with the epicentre of the world's fastest-growing Ebola outbreak, that is an impressive result, and it reflects Uganda's experience — it has handled multiple Ebola outbreaks before and had the playbook ready.

## Part 4: Why North Kivu has become the hardest place

In early October, Médecins Sans Frontières — Doctors Without Borders — published a statement describing their work in North Kivu's city of Butembo as **fighting a megafire.** The phrase comes from Stéphanie Hoffmann, who coordinates their Ebola treatment centre there.

Her account, and the figures MSF published alongside it, explain what has gone wrong in that province.

- North Kivu is now generating **about 40% of all new confirmed cases nationally — up from 24% at the end of August.**
- As of late September, **34% of confirmed Ebola patients were being cared for in ordinary, non-specialised health facilities** rather than in proper treatment centres.
- **60% of Ebola deaths are still happening in the community** rather than in treatment centres.
- Butembo is a city of roughly **2 million people**. MSF's treatment centre there has **29 beds.** Two nearby transit centres have 16 and 5.

That last comparison is the whole problem in one line. Fifty beds for two million people, in the province generating the most new cases in the country.

### Why so many people die at home

That 60% figure is the most revealing number in this outbreak, and it is worth sitting with.

When most Ebola deaths happen at home rather than in treatment centres, it means people are not coming in — or are coming in far too late to be helped. That has several causes layered on top of each other:

- **Capacity.** You cannot go to a treatment centre with no free bed. MSF reports that for weeks they have sometimes had to send confirmed patients elsewhere.
- **Distance and cost.** Reaching a centre from a rural district is expensive and slow.
- **Fear, grounded in experience.** Going into isolation means separation from family, possibly to die alone. People watch neighbours enter treatment centres and not come out — which, with a 48% fatality rate, happens roughly half the time even with the best available care.
- **History.** North Kivu was the centre of Congo's 2018–20 Ebola outbreak. That response was marred by documented abuses, and local people have not forgotten.

And here is the loop that makes it so hard to break: **deaths at home are far more likely to infect other people**, because families care for the dying and prepare the dead. So avoiding treatment centres causes more infections, which causes more deaths, which confirms the belief that the epidemic is uncontrollable and the centres are not helping.

For context on progress: that community-death figure was 66–70% in August. It is 60% now. Two months of maximum international attention has moved it by single digits.

### Violence, and a treatment centre burned down

Late September and early October were the worst stretch of this outbreak for attacks on the response itself.

- **22 September:** a local political official was beaten by residents in Butembo after taking part in Ebola awareness work, and later died.
- **26 September:** a worker at a screening post outside Butembo was shot dead.
- **27–28 September:** two Ebola response workers were killed in North Kivu.
- **30 September:** the **Ebola transit centre in the Kigonze displacement camp near Bunia was destroyed by fire**, set by soldiers searching for weapons. **About 19,000 people fled the camp.**
- **From 27 September:** more than 5,000 people fled fighting in Ituri, over 3,000 from a single displacement site. At least four people were killed in Djugu. **Response operations were suspended entirely in three health districts**, with laboratory samples from one of them delayed in transit.

The Kigonze fire is the one that most needs explaining, because it was not community resistance to the Ebola response. It was a **national army operation** that destroyed Ebola infrastructure and displaced 19,000 people from a camp in the middle of an epidemic. The response in eastern Congo is not simply fighting a virus and public mistrust. It is operating inside an active armed conflict involving multiple parties, including the government's own forces.

### Armed enforcement

In August, authorities in Butembo created a task force drawn from **Wazalendo** pro-government militia fighters, announced as a measure to protect health workers and facilities, patrol treatment centres, and discourage unsafe handling of bodies. A spokesman warned that "constraining measures" could follow if people did not observe 21-day quarantine.

By late September, armed men were operating handwashing stations on roads into Butembo. There are reports that a commander threatened to forcibly take people to treatment centres and to whip those who refused, though that specific allegation comes from a single source and should be treated as unconfirmed.

Both local civil society and WHO have flagged the obvious risk: **using armed men to enforce public health measures in a community that already distrusts the response is likely to deepen the distrust, not reduce it.** The same dynamic in 2018–20 is part of why North Kivu is as difficult as it is today.

### The surveillance problem, stated plainly

This is a figure that deserves more attention than it has received.

Congo's response reports that **78.7% of identified contacts of Ebola patients are being followed up.** That sounds like a functioning system. But that 78.7% applies to a list that captures only about **23% of the contacts you would expect** a caseload of this size to generate.

And the Africa CDC's director-general, Jean Kaseya, reports that **more than 70% of new cases are being found in the community among people who were never on a contact list at all.**

Put together: the response is following most of a list that is missing three-quarters of the people it should contain, and is finding most new patients outside that list entirely. **That is not contact tracing in the sense epidemiologists mean it.** Contact tracing works by knowing in advance who has been exposed, watching them, and isolating them the moment they become infectious. What is happening instead is case-finding — discovering infections after the fact, often after they have already spread.

Africa CDC attributes this to insecurity and community resistance in several health districts, which is consistent with everything above.

## Part 5: Children

The UN children's agency briefed journalists on 6 October with figures that had not previously been assembled in public.

- **More than 1,900 children** have lost one or both parents or caregivers, or been separated from them, since May — **1,581 in Ituri and 322 in North Kivu.**
- **2,076 children** have been confirmed infected; **1,162 have died.**
- Children are **just over a quarter of confirmed cases but about a third of deaths.**
- **Roughly seven in ten confirmed cases in children under five have died.**
- UNICEF has asked for **US$113.4 million** and received just over a third of it.
- Twelve nurseries operate nationwide for children whose caregivers are sick or dead. UNICEF wants one at every treatment centre.

The under-five figure is the most severe statistic in this outbreak from any source, and it is invisible in the headline 48% rate. Part of the explanation is medical — small children tolerate fluid loss poorly. Part is diagnostic: WHO's incident manager has noted that infections in very young children are especially hard to detect, because they cannot describe their symptoms and the presentation is easy to confuse with other illnesses. By the time the diagnosis is made, it is often too late.

## Part 6: What is being done

This is where the picture is genuinely better than it was three months ago, and it is the part of the story that gets least coverage.

### Testing: the biggest fix, already delivered

As explained in Part 1, the most widely deployed Ebola test in the region **could not detect this virus** — a catastrophic starting position.

**That is now solved.** On **3 September 2026, WHO authorised Cepheid's Xpert Hemorrhagic Fever Panel** for emergency use. It detects Ebola across all four species that infect humans — including Bundibugyo — plus Marburg, Crimean-Congo haemorrhagic fever, and Lassa fever. Crucially, **it runs on the GeneXpert machines already installed across central and east Africa**, and gives results in about an hour.

The practical effect is that the region's existing laboratory equipment went from useless for this outbreak to usable, overnight, without buying new instruments.

The first laboratory test for Bundibugyo had been authorised earlier, on **2 July**, with a two-to-three-hour turnaround. Three molecular tests held emergency authorisation by late August, with more under review.

Two honest limitations. **There is still no rapid "finger-prick" antigen test** for this virus — everything available requires a laboratory machine. And one published study documented a different commercial test returning invalid results on three samples that genome sequencing later confirmed were positive. No systematic study of how well current tests detect the specific virus circulating this year has been published.

### Vaccines: three candidates in human trials

There is still **no licensed or emergency-authorised vaccine for Bundibugyo virus.** But the development response has been genuinely fast.

**What is being used now.** The licensed Zaire vaccine, Ervebo, is being given to health workers and frontline responders — **about 7,366 people as of early October**. WHO's guidance, issued 31 August, is explicit that Ervebo should be used **only inside research protocols, not mass campaigns**, because it is genuinely unknown whether it protects against this species. WHO has called for a properly randomised trial to settle the question. A large effectiveness study of **20,000 frontline workers** across Ituri and North Kivu launched on 19 September; by its own design it cannot produce results before mid-2027.

**The supply problem.** An international allocation body approved **70,000 Ervebo doses** in August. By mid-September, **only 16,520 had physically arrived in Congo.** One province was reported to have run out of investigational vaccine entirely in late September.

That matters because of what surveys show about demand — see Part 8. The constraint on vaccination right now is **getting doses into the country**, not persuading people to take them.

**What is in development specifically for this virus:**

- **An Oxford University vaccine** (ChAdOx1 BDBV), manufactured by the Serum Institute of India. First volunteer vaccinated in Oxford on **24 July** — eight weeks from concept to clinic. A second trial **began in Uganda on 6 October.** The Serum Institute has already manufactured **620,000 doses at its own risk**, before knowing whether the vaccine works.
- **A Moderna mRNA vaccine** (mRNA-1469). First participant dosed in Canada on **3 August**, about 80 healthy adults across three sites. Moderna has committed at least 500,000 doses to lower-income countries at access pricing if it is licensed.
- **An Egyptian-German candidate** (Minapharm/ProBioGen), funded in August to move toward a trial in Africa, with eventual manufacturing in Cairo.
- **Two further candidates** from IAVI and Public Health Vaccines are in earlier development and not yet in humans.

None has published results yet. But the pipeline went from nothing to three candidates in human trials in under three months, which is historically very fast.

### Treatments: two proper trials are enrolling

There is no approved treatment for Bundibugyo virus. There are two randomised clinical trials running, which is the right way to find out whether something works rather than guessing.

**PARTNERS** is run by WHO with Congo's national biomedical research institute, the Institute of Tropical Medicine in Antwerp, and Oxford University, delivered alongside MSF and ALIMA. It tests an **antibody treatment called MBP134** and the antiviral **remdesivir** — each alone, both together, and against standard supportive care. Open to patients of any age. It aims for 700–1,000 participants and had enrolled **over 300 across five treatment facilities by early September.**

**EBO-PEP BUNDI** tests whether an oral antiviral, **obeldesivir**, can prevent illness in people who have already been exposed — about 1,000 high-risk contacts, monitored daily for 21 days. First participant enrolled 16 July.

**MBP134** is worth a note. It is a two-antibody combination derived from a survivor of the West Africa epidemic and engineered to work across multiple Ebola species. In animal studies it protected against Bundibugyo even when given a full week after infection. It has never been proven effective in humans — that is what PARTNERS is for — but it is also being provided outside the trial, on a compassionate basis, to people with high-risk exposures.

### Tracking the virus genetically

Scientists have sequenced the virus's genome extensively, and the findings are more reassuring than people often assume.

A study published in *Nature Medicine* in August established that the 2026 virus is **genetically distinct from the 2007 and 2012 Bundibugyo outbreaks** — meaning this is a new jump from an animal reservoir, most likely bats, rather than a resurgence of an old strain.

The largest analysis to date, covering **525 genomes** collected between May and August, traced the outbreak's probable origin to the mining town of Mongbwalu and dated the common ancestor of all sequenced cases to around **22 February 2026** — nearly three months before the outbreak was officially declared.

**Importantly: there is no evidence the virus is becoming more dangerous or more transmissible.** That large analysis found genetic diversity increasing as expected, with **no sign of mutations that change the virus's fitness** and no single variant taking over. The mutations that have been found are mostly the signature of ordinary human-to-human transmission, not adaptation. If you see a claim that this virus is mutating into something worse, it is not supported by the published evidence.

One structural problem is worth naming. Of roughly 648 genome sequences from this outbreak deposited in the main public database, only **about 5% are fully open**. The remaining 95% carry restrictions requiring co-authorship or written permission before they can be used in a publication. There are reasonable equity arguments for those terms — researchers in affected countries have historically had their samples used without credit. But the practical effect is that independent scientists cannot freely analyse most of the data from the outbreak.

## Part 7: Money, and where it is not going

### What has been pledged

A great deal — though the exact figure depends on who is counting.

At a **G20 health ministers' meeting on 30 September**, $470 million in new pledges was announced, bringing the cumulative total since May to **more than $2 billion**. The donor list: United States $267 million (bringing its total to **$887 million**), World Bank $90 million, UK $67.5 million, Germany $24.8 million, South Korea $18 million, France $16 million, Italy $11.3 million, UAE $10 million, Japan $5 million, Australia $3.5 million.

Separately, Africa CDC has described a figure closer to **$2.9 billion** mobilised. The two totals probably count different things — one health-specific pledges at a single meeting, the other including broader humanitarian and multi-year commitments — and neither should be treated as definitive.

Other contributions include the UK's £78.7 million cumulative, EU commitments of €15 million in humanitarian aid plus €2.5 million in diagnostic tests plus a further €7.5 million, an additional €7 million from France announced on 6 October, $15 million from the Gates Foundation, and $40 million from the vaccine alliance Gavi.

### The gap between pledged and spent

Here is the figure that cuts through all of it.

Reporting from early October describes **Congo's own government having spent about $15 million, against roughly $600 million from international partners** — the latter largely controlled by international NGOs rather than funding local staff.

Against $2 billion or more in pledges, $600 million is the operative number. A pledge is an announcement. Disbursement is a separate, slower, much less publicised process, and no organisation has published a reconciliation of what has been promised against what has actually been spent. Africa CDC is now publicly demanding exactly that, with its director-general insisting every dollar be traceable from commitment to delivery.

### The thing that has not been fixed

**Congolese health workers responding to this outbreak have not been paid.**

Strikes have recurred since July across multiple treatment centres. In one town, staff were owed three months of wages and saw their hazard pay cut from $25 a day to $12. The government began disbursing bonuses at the end of July. **As of 2 October, surveillance staff and others were still unpaid for August and September and were protesting.**

This is, by a wide margin, the clearest failure in the entire response, and it is worth being blunt about why.

WHO has reported treatment beds being added across the country — roughly 1,600 beds across about 50 centres, up from around 1,000 in August against an estimated 3,000 needed. But **a bed without a nurse is not a treatment bed.** WHO's own incident manager has identified staffing, not beds, as one of the largest gaps in the response. Fifty health workers have died. Those who remain have gone two months without pay while being asked to treat a disease that kills half the people who catch it.

Clearing those arrears would cost a rounding error against $887 million from the United States alone. It has not happened in the months it has been obviously necessary.

## Part 8: Trust, and what people actually believe

Much reporting on Ebola in Congo emphasises community mistrust, conspiracy theories, and resistance to the response. All of that is real and documented. But the public picture is often more extreme than the evidence supports, and the actual data is worth seeing.

### What surveys find

The most-cited study is from the previous outbreak: a **survey of 961 adults in Beni and Butembo, North Kivu, in September 2018**, published in *The Lancet Infectious Diseases*. Its findings:

- **25% believed Ebola was not real.**
- 33% thought authorities had fabricated it for financial gain.
- 36% thought it had been fabricated to destabilise the region.
- 19% held all three beliefs.
- Fewer than 32% trusted local authorities to represent their interests.
- **Health workers were rated more trustworthy than government officials.**

That is a serious misinformation problem affecting a substantial minority. It is not a collapse of shared reality, and the single most trusted group was health workers.

The only quantitative survey from 2026 points the same direction, more strongly. Fielded nationwide between July and September across all 26 provinces, with 1,516 respondents:

- **96%** had heard of the outbreak.
- **74% said they would take a vaccine** if one were available.
- **88%** said they would accept a trained burial team.
- **28%** believed the virus was artificially created by foreigners.
- Only **20%** knew that survivors can transmit the virus through certain bodily fluids well after recovery.
- **49%** would feel uncomfortable greeting someone who had recovered.
- **Health workers were again the single most trusted information source.**
- People holding the lab-creation belief were **three times more likely to refuse vaccination and five times more likely to seek informal care.**

Two caveats: this survey is a market-research publication rather than a peer-reviewed study, and it is nationwide rather than focused on the worst-affected districts, where attitudes are very likely worse than the national average. **No peer-reviewed survey of trust or beliefs specific to Ituri or North Kivu in 2026 has been published** — a genuine gap in what anyone knows.

### What this means practically

Three things follow.

**First, the demand problem is not what it appears.** Set 74% stated willingness to be vaccinated against 16,520 doses physically delivered out of 70,000 approved. The bottleneck in October 2026 is logistics, not persuasion. That is a much better problem to have and a completely different one to solve.

**Second, the real knowledge gap is specific and fixable.** Only one in five people knows that survivors can transmit the virus after recovery. That is not a conspiracy belief — it is a straightforward information deficit, and it affects both stigma against survivors and actual transmission risk.

**Third, mistrust where it exists has reasons.** Researchers who study this consistently locate it in history rather than irrationality: a long record of outside institutions arriving during crises and leaving afterwards, inadequate protection during conflict, and documented abuses during the 2018–20 response. The phrase that circulates locally is that *"Ebola is a business"* — a criticism of how resources flow during epidemics and vanish between them. Whatever one makes of it, it is a conclusion drawn from experience, not from ignorance.

The deepest measure of trust in this outbreak is not any survey. It is that **60% of deaths still occur at home.** People are voting with their feet, and they are voting against the treatment centres.

## Part 9: The international picture

### Formal emergency status

WHO declared this outbreak a **Public Health Emergency of International Concern** in mid-May 2026 — its highest formal alarm, the same designation used for COVID-19 and for the 2014 West Africa epidemic.

The expert committee advising on that status has met twice, most recently on **18 August**. It concluded that the outbreak **remains** an international emergency but **does not meet the criteria for a "pandemic emergency"**, a higher category — with 11 of 12 members agreeing.

That committee's August assessment is the most authoritative statement available on how much of this outbreak is actually being seen. Against roughly 5,000 reported cases at the time, it judged the **true burden to be three to four times higher.** It warned the outbreak could exceed the West Africa epidemic without a change in approach, and recommended drastically scaling up infection control, isolation capacity, community engagement, and a single costed national plan.

**No third meeting has been convened, and no formal review has been triggered by the Kenya case.** There has also been no WHO situation update published since 25 September, which means there is currently no WHO-authoritative case count that postdates the export to Kenya.

### Regional response

Africa CDC and WHO launched a joint continental plan in June — **$518 million over six months**, covering surveillance, laboratories, infection control, clinical care, community engagement, research and logistics, focused on Congo and Uganda plus ten neighbouring countries judged at highest risk. That plan's window closes in November. No revised version has been published, though cases have roughly doubled since it was written.

Neighbouring countries are preparing. **South Sudan** has activated its response measures and has no confirmed cases. The **Central African Republic** has hosted cross-border planning and has no cases. Rwanda, Burundi, Tanzania and Congo-Brazzaville have preparedness activity and no cases. **Nigeria** strengthened its surveillance and ran a national exercise after the Kenya case, and its main airport began enforcing health declaration forms.

The US CDC bars travellers who have been in Congo within the previous 21 days from boarding US-bound flights, with arrivals from Uganda or South Sudan routed to designated airports for screening.

## Part 10: A note on measles in the United States

This is a separate situation, but it belongs in any honest account of how outbreaks are going in 2026, and it is a useful comparison.

| | |
|---|---|
| **Confirmed US measles cases in 2026** | **3,887** |
| **Increase over the previous week** | **+228 (+6.2%)** |
| **Separate outbreaks this year** | 42 |
| **Share of cases linked to an outbreak** | 95% |
| **Share hospitalised** | 9% |
| **Jurisdictions reporting cases** | 47 |

As of 1 October. This is the eighth consecutive week of triple-digit weekly increases, and the highest annual total since the United States declared measles eliminated in 2000 — in fact the highest since 1991.

**Pennsylvania** is the epicentre, with 977 cases and 195 hospitalisations.

One detail worth understanding, because it causes confusion. **The federal CDC count shows 2 measles deaths this year. Pennsylvania's state health department has confirmed 5.** Both are correct within their own systems: CDC waits for confirmation through national vital-statistics records, which is slower and stricter, while the state counts its own confirmed cases. Nobody is hiding anything. But a federal dashboard showing 2 while a state reports 5 is corrosive to public confidence regardless of which methodology is more defensible — and that is exactly the kind of small institutional friction that, scaled up and sustained, produces the trust problems described in Part 8.

The comparison with Congo is instructive in one direction only. Measles is far more contagious than Ebola — among the most contagious diseases known — but it has a safe, cheap, extremely effective vaccine that has existed for sixty years. Kindergarten vaccination coverage has fallen below the level needed for community protection in most US states. **Congo is fighting a disease with no vaccine. The United States is losing ground against one it solved decades ago.**

## Part 11: What to watch

If you want to follow this outbreak without tracking every daily figure, three numbers tell you most of what is happening.

**1. The share of deaths occurring at home.** It was 66–70% in August and 60% in early October. If it keeps falling, people are reaching care earlier and the central failure mode is easing. If it stalls, nothing else much matters.

**2. North Kivu's share of new cases nationally.** It was 24% at the end of August and about 40% in early October. This tells you where the epidemic is heading rather than where it has been.

**3. The ratio of contacts traced to contacts expected** — currently about 23%. This is the clearest measure of whether the response can see what it is fighting. Until it rises substantially, the response is finding cases after transmission rather than preventing it.

A fourth, if you can find it: **whether health workers get paid.** It is the cheapest, most obviously necessary, and most persistently unmade investment in this entire response.

### What would count as good news

- The community-death share falling below 50%.
- Ervebo deliveries reaching the full 70,000 approved doses.
- A properly randomised vaccine trial launching, which would finally answer whether the existing vaccine helps against this species.
- Results from the PARTNERS treatment trial, which could give clinicians the first proven therapy for this virus.
- Arrears cleared and strikes ended.

### What would count as bad news

- A confirmed case in Kinshasa, which would put the virus in a city of 17 million with international air links.
- Sustained transmission in Kenya, South Sudan, or any neighbouring country, as opposed to isolated imported cases.
- Further destruction of treatment facilities, from any cause.
- The under-five fatality rate staying near 70%.

## Where the record is unclear

Worth knowing, so you can read coverage of this outbreak critically.

- **Case totals differ between sources** by more than a day's worth of cases. WHO, the Congolese government and the US CDC publish separate figures that lag each other.
- **Weekly trend figures from different organisations are not comparable** and should not be combined.
- **No WHO situation update has been published since 25 September**, so there is no WHO-confirmed count that includes the Kenya case.
- **The two funding totals in circulation** — above $2 billion and about $2.9 billion — cannot be reconciled from public sources, and the meeting at which the pledges were made is dated variously 23, 25 and 30 September.
- **No reconciliation of money pledged against money spent** has been published by anyone.
- **The Kenya patient's exact departure point in Congo** is reported by only one outlet.
- **The timeline of his laboratory confirmation and his death** is internally inconsistent in official statements and has not been clarified.
- **No Ugandan contact-tracing figures** have been published for his road journey through Kampala.
- **Whether Congolese health workers' back pay has been settled** could not be confirmed; the most recent information is that they were unpaid for two months as of 2 October.
- **The allegation of whipping and forced hospitalisation** by militia enforcing quarantine comes from a single source and is unconfirmed.
- **No peer-reviewed survey of community attitudes in the worst-affected provinces in 2026** exists.
- **The date the international emergency was declared** is given as 15, 16 or 17 May depending on the source, and the date Uganda's outbreak ended as 25, 26 or 27 August.
